# Module 1: Understanding Agentic AI

**Author:** Alexander Booth
**Date:** July 2026
**Cohort:** MIT-AGAI July 20, 2026

## Overview

Module 1 establishes what agentic AI actually is before the program moves to how organizations should adopt and govern it. It places agentic AI on the broader **AI continuum** — classical machine learning, deep learning, generative AI, agentic AI, and (eventually) AGI — and draws the module's central distinction: **reactive** systems that take a prompt and produce a response, versus **proactive** systems that take a goal and produce a plan, an execution, and an adaptation. It then goes under the hood of that shift, unpacking the architecture (tool use, planning and task decomposition, memory, feedback loops) that turns a large language model into an agent, before pivoting to the leadership question of where this creates real organizational value, where it doesn't yet, and what has to be true of an organization before it can absorb it. The module closes with a serialized case narrative — a fictionalized industrial company, Boulton Industries, working through exactly these questions in three weekly leadership meetings — that dramatizes the module's abstractions as a live leadership problem.

## Why This Module Matters

"Agent" is what Marvin Minsky called a **suitcase word** — a term people pack with different meanings and carry around as if it were one thing. Before an organization can evaluate, adopt, or govern agentic AI, its leaders need to be using the word to mean the same thing. Skipping past that shared vocabulary produces the appearance of alignment without the substance of it: six executives can agree enthusiastically to "invest in agents" while each is picturing a different architecture, a different accountability model, and a different deployment. This module exists to build that shared vocabulary and, just as importantly, to calibrate expectations — today's agentic systems are genuinely useful but still brittle, still require guardrails, and still need human oversight at critical junctures. Understanding both the opportunity and the constraint is what separates a leader who can evaluate an AI initiative from one who simply approves a budget line.

## What This Module Covers

1. **The AI continuum and the reactive-to-proactive shift.** Classical ML and deep learning classify, predict, and respond within tightly defined boundaries. Generative AI is fluent but still **reactive**: you prompt it, it responds; it doesn't set its own goals or decide which tools to use. Agentic AI is a **qualitative threshold**, not a quantitative step further along the same line — it is **proactive**: given an objective, it plans steps, selects tools, executes, checks its own work, adjusts course, and escalates to a human when it hits a wall. The recurring analogy: a large language model is a research assistant (or an expert witness — brilliant when asked the right question, but not running the case); an agentic system is a capable project manager running the case.

2. **What turns an LLM into an agent.** An LLM on its own is a next-token prediction engine — one input, one output, no goals, no memory across turns. Wrapping it in a system that gives it **tool access** (databases, APIs, search, code execution), **planning and task decomposition** (breaking a complex objective into ordered subtasks), **memory and context management** (maintaining state across a multi-step workflow), and **feedback and self-correction** (evaluating and revising its own output, e.g. via chain-of-thought reasoning) is what makes it agentic. The relationship between LLM and agent is augmentation, not replacement: the LLM is the reasoning engine, the surrounding architecture is the scaffolding that makes it operationally useful.

3. **The five building blocks of agent design.** Every agent design is a set of commitments across five axes: **perception** (how it takes input from the world), **action** (how it writes down and executes actions, typically as small programs that call external tools), **planning** (reasoning about the long-term consequences of actions before taking them — including "thinking step by step" and simulating possible future outcomes when a simulator is available), **memory/learning** (improving from experience after deployment), and **safety** (architecting the system to limit harmful side effects and give humans review opportunities before consequential actions are taken).

4. **Where the value sits, and where the constraints are.** Agentic AI is most compelling for workflows that are multi-step, cross-functional, and involve judgment across a chain of decisions (e.g., insurance claims processing, supply chain monitoring, customer complaint resolution) — not single-task outputs. Its value isn't in replacing human judgment; it's in amplifying it by absorbing high-volume cognitive work so human attention concentrates on the decisions that actually require it. The constraint is symmetric: today's agentic systems remain brittle in unstructured environments and perform best where the problem space is well defined, tools are well integrated, and guardrails are explicit — autonomy without structure is risk. Because a system that can act (query a database, send an email, trigger a purchase order) compounds the cost of errors beyond what a reactive chatbot's mistakes would cost, the leadership question is not "can we make this agentic" but **where does autonomy add value, and where does it add risk**.

5. **The agentic workflow as the unit of deployment.** No organization deploys a building block — it deploys a workflow. An agentic AI workflow is a structured, multi-step chain of reasoning, actions, and decisions that an agent (or system of agents) executes with a degree of autonomy, analogous to giving a colleague a project brief rather than asking them a single question. Much of the value organizations seek from AI lives not in any single task but in the "connective tissue" between tasks — the handoffs and judgment calls that link one step to the next. Reimagining a workflow with agentic capabilities available from the start is a fundamentally different exercise than automating an existing one by removing the human.

6. **Three adoption realities and the operationalization gap.** (1) Speed of iteration matters more than perfection of plan — a long evaluation cycle is itself a form of risk in a fast-moving landscape. (2) The biggest barriers to adoption are organizational, not technical — siloed teams, misaligned incentives, and underestimated change management stall deployments more often than model capability does. (3) Existing KPIs may not capture the new forms of value (or risk) agentic systems create. Operationalizing agentic workflows inside a real organization surfaces three further gaps: a **definitional** gap (the field moves so fast that what counted as "agentic" six months ago may now be a standard model feature), an **organizational readiness** gap (agentic workflows cross functional boundaries and require data and collaboration patterns many organizations don't have), and a **pace** gap (deciding what "responsible speed" looks like when neither recklessness nor excessive caution serves the organization well).

7. **The moving target of "agent" itself.** Capabilities once associated with agent scaffolding — tool/function calling, reasoning, memory — are increasingly being absorbed directly into the underlying models, shifting the practical challenge toward orchestration, infrastructure, and deployment ("the plumbing"). The genuine frontier beyond today's single-agent workflows is the **swarm of agents**: specialized, collaborative agents that can create other agents and act with increasing autonomy — technically emerging but not yet operationally mature, and worth watching even where an organization isn't ready to bet on it.

  * *Video 1* (`MIT AGAI M1U2 Media Set Video 1 Transcript.pdf`) — Distinguishes language models (which take instructions and produce data/text) from language model agents (a language model equipped with scaffolding to perceive and act in the world via programs it writes).   * *Video 2* (`MIT AGAI M1U2 Media Set Video 2 Transcript.pdf`) — Walks through building a customer service assistant step by step: why stuffing all company knowledge into a prompt fails (security and cost), and how giving the model callable tools (`search_help`, `search_orders`, `send_message`) turns its text output into programs that retrieve information and eventually produce real side effects (e.g., printing a return label).
  * *Video 3* (`MIT AGAI M1U2 Media Set Video 3 Transcript.pdf`) — Covers more advanced agent capabilities: chain-of-thought ("think step by step") reasoning for more reliable answers, offloading reasoning to code (e.g., date math) rather than relying on the model's internal knowledge, and planning by simulating possible future outcomes in parallel before committing to a sequence of actions — noting this only works with access to a simulator or reversible actions.

* **U3 — Video** (`MIT AGAI M1U3 Video 1 Transcript.pdf`): McDonagh-Smith shifts the lens from agent building blocks to the **agentic AI workflow** as the real unit of organizational deployment — the structured, multi-step chain of reasoning, actions, and decisions an agent executes with autonomy, illustrated via a customer complaint-handling process. Names the "translation problem" between understanding workflows conceptually and operationalizing them: a definitional challenge (shared vocabulary), an organizational readiness challenge (cross-functional data and collaboration), and a pace challenge (what responsible speed looks like).

* **U4 — Podcast** (`MIT AGAI M1U4 Podcast 1 Transcript.pdf`): A narrative case study — McDonagh-Smith recounts sitting in on three weekly leadership meetings at "Boulton Industries," a fictionalized industrial company responding to a board mandate on agentic AI. The first meeting (covered in this transcript) opens with the CEO asking each of six executives to define "agent" in one sentence — surfacing five genuinely different definitions and the suitcase-word problem — then works through the AI continuum, a three-wave model of AI adoption (virtual agents/chatbots, internal productivity, autonomous action), an honest self-assessment of where Boulton sits on each wave ("ambitious but unprepared" on wave three), and the same three adoption realities from U1 Video 3, reframing the board's question from "should we adopt agentic AI" to "what kind of organization do we need to become to deploy it well."

## Key Takeaways

* Agentic AI is a **qualitative shift**, not an incremental one — from systems that respond to prompts to systems that pursue goals through planning, tool use, memory, and self-correction.
* "Agent" is a suitcase word; before evaluating or adopting agentic AI, an organization needs a **shared definition**, or it will mistake surface-level agreement for real alignment.
* Value concentrates in **multi-step, cross-functional workflows** with judgment chains, not single-task outputs — and the workflow, not the isolated agent capability, is the real unit of deployment.
* Today's agentic systems are genuinely useful but still **brittle**: they perform best in well-defined problem spaces with integrated tools and explicit guardrails, and autonomy without structure is risk.
* The biggest barriers to adoption are consistently **organizational, not technical** — siloed teams, misaligned incentives, and underdeveloped data plumbing, not model capability.
* The definition of "agent" itself keeps moving as capabilities (reasoning, memory, tool-calling) migrate from external scaffolding into the models — leaders should track the frontier (e.g., swarms of collaborative agents) without over-indexing strategy on any one snapshot of the technology.
